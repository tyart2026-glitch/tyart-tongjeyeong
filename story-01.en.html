STORY 01 · HISTORY

The Naval Headquarters That Guarded the Waters of Three Provinces:
Where Tongyeong Began

Where the name Tongyeong began

Follow the name Tongyeong back in time and you arrive at a military base that once stood here: Samdo Sugun Tongjeyeong (三道水軍統制營), the headquarters that commanded the naval forces of the three provinces of Chungcheong, Jeolla and Gyeongsang. The city’s present name, Tongyeong, began as a shortened form of the command’s name, Tongjeyeong.

Tongyeong’s history begins at sea. In 1592 the Joseon navy defeated the Japanese fleet off Hansando Island, and the following year the court created the post of Samdo Sugun Tongjesa, placing the naval forces of all three provinces under a single commander. The first to hold it was Yi Sun-sin. His base on Hansando is regarded as the first Tongjeyeong.

The command was never simply a place where soldiers were stationed. Defending a wide stretch of sea meant not only leading the fleet but also securing provisions, producing weapons and supplies, and managing everything the base needed to run. Waging war required systems for handling food, clothing, ships and equipment as much as it required weapons. The history of Tongjeyeong is the history of naval operations, and equally of the administration and supply that made them possible.

From the camp on Hansando to the base at Duryongpo

The command was not always based in present-day Tongyeong. After the Hansando camp was destroyed during the second Japanese invasion of 1597 (the Jeongyu War), the command moved several times, with stops at Oapo on Geojedo Island and Chunwonpo in Goseong, among others. Even after the war ended, the naval headquarters went on searching for a permanent home.

The turning point came in 1603. The sixth commander, Yi Gyeong-jun, chose Duryongpo, now the Munhwa-dong area of Tongyeong, as the site of the command and began construction. In 1605 the main buildings followed, including Sebyeonggwan Hall, the official guesthouse, as well as Baekhwadang Hall and Jeonghaejeong Pavilion. Once the headquarters had settled at Duryongpo, the area grew into a place where naval command, administration and supply came together.

The command at Duryongpo was never a single building. By the mid-nineteenth century, more than a hundred government buildings are said to have stood around Sebyeonggwan. Together they formed an interconnected complex for command and administration, the management of supplies and provisions, and a wide range of duties and ceremonies. Tongjeyeong fulfilled its military purpose while also serving as the heart of a town where people and goods gathered and circulated.

Keeping such a large base running required people with specialist skills. Artisans who made weapons, worked leather, or produced fans, hats, furniture and metal fittings all supported its operations. Their work started out serving military needs, but as the range of goods required widened, it grew into a craft industry that also turned out everyday wares and tribute items for the king.

That crafts flourished as the command grew is key to understanding Tongyeong’s history. The base gave artisans a purpose for their craft and a steady stream of work, and they, in turn, used their skills to meet the base’s everyday needs. Military affairs, administration, logistics and craft were not separate worlds but different functions that kept one place running.

292 years,
and the name that remained

The command at Duryongpo was abolished in 1895. For 292 years, from the start of construction here in 1603 until its closure, Tongjeyeong was woven into the history of this place. This figure counts only the years at present-day Tongyeong; the institution itself and the camp on Hansando go back further to 1593.

Not every trace vanished when the command closed its doors. During the Japanese colonial period most of its buildings were torn down, but Sebyeonggwan Hall survived, an important piece of architectural heritage that still conveys what the old base looked like. Surveys, conservation and restoration work followed, and visitors can now explore Tongjeyeong through Sebyeonggwan, several restored buildings and archaeological remains. The Tongjeyeong you see today is a historic site where the surviving original hall stands alongside excavated foundations and reconstructions.

And so the city’s name lived on. The two syllables of Tongyeong carry the name of the naval headquarters, and layered within that name are the years from the camp on Hansando to the base at Duryongpo, along with the lives of the many people who worked there.

Now walk the paths of Tongjeyeong. The wide wooden floor of Sebyeonggwan Hall and the buildings along the workshop street may look like separate places, but they were all part of a single system that kept the base running. Once you understand how the naval headquarters worked, the objects its artisans made and the skills behind them take on new meaning.

“The name Tongyeong holds both the years when the naval headquarters stood here and the lives of the people who worked in it.”
